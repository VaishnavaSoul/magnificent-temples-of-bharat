Overview

The Parthasarathy Temple in Triplicane (Thiruvallikeni), Chennai, Tamil Nadu, India, is one of the city’s oldest and most significant Vaishnavite temples, with origins dating back to the 6th century CE under the patronage of the Pallava dynasty. Recognised as the 61st of the 108 Divya Desams, this sacred shrine is revered in the Naalayira Divya Prabandham — the canonical Tamil devotional hymns of the Alvar saints — and has continued to be a vital centre of Vishnu worship for well over a millennium. Its name, Parthasarathy, literally means “charioteer of Partha (Arjuna),” celebrating Lord Krishna’s role as Arjuna’s guide and charioteer in the Mahabharata, and visitors are drawn here not only for spiritual solace but also for the temple’s deep connections with ancient Tamil religious literature and tradition.

Sthala Purana (Legend and Spiritual Narrative)

According to legend, the Parthasarathy Temple’s origins are tied to the story of King Sumati, a devout follower of Vishnu who yearned to see the Lord in the very form He took during the Kurukshetra war — as the charioteer of Arjuna. Pleased by the king’s devotion, Vishnu appeared to him in a dream as Parthasarathy and promised to reside in that very form at this sacred location. This divine appearance blessed the site with everlasting sanctity and established the temple’s identity as a place where devotees can seek guidance, courage, and inner strength, reflecting Krishna’s role as spiritual guide in the Bhagavad Gita.

Moolavar and Temple Deities

The central deity (moolavar) of the temple is Lord Vishnu as Parthasarathy (Krishna), uniquely depicted with a moustache, a rare iconographic form that symbolises Krishna’s valor as a warrior riding Arjuna’s chariot in the Mahabharata. He traditionally holds only the conch (shankha) and not the discus (chakra), in keeping with the vow taken during the war not to wield weapons. Surrounding the sanctum are remarkable shrines dedicated to several divine forms, including Sri Ranganatha (reclining Vishnu), Sri Rama, Sri Narasimha (Yoga Narasimha), and Sri Gajendra Varadaraja, alongside the consort Sri Vedavalli Thayar. The temple also honours Alvars and notable Vaishnavite acharyas, creating a sacred space where multiple manifestations of Vishnu and his devotees converge in one complex.

Peacock Legends and Temple Origins

One of the most unique aspects of the Kapaleeshwarar Temple is its deep connection with the peacock (mayil in Tamil) symbolism, which is rarely seen as a central myth in other Shaiva temples. According to legend, Goddess Parvati once worshipped Lord Shiva here in the form of a peahen to regain her divine form after a curse, an act demonstrating humility, devotion, and the transformative power of grace. This myth is so central to the temple’s identity that the neighbourhood of Mylapore itself derives its name from the peacock, linking the temple’s spiritual legacy with the city’s historical and cultural identity. Inside the temple, representations of peacocks and peahens in sculptures, reliefs, and even ritual motifs commemorate this story, creating a visual narrative that is unique in both architectural symbolism and devotional practice. Unlike most Shiva temples, where animal symbolism exists but rarely dominates, here the peacock’s sacred association with Parvati worship is celebrated in daily rituals, festival decorations, and the temple’s very spatial organization, making it a singular feature of Kapaleeshwarar’s mythic and devotional heritage.

The Temple Tank and Theppa Festival: A Living Ritual

Another distinguishing feature is the Kapaleeshwarar Temple Tank, a large sacred water body situated west of the main shrine, which is not just decorative but central to the temple’s ritual life. During the Theppa Thiruvizha (Float Festival), the deity is placed on a beautifully decorated raft that floats on the tank, a spectacle unmatched in most Shiva temples in urban settings. The tank itself, historically linked to sacred purification and community gathering, allows devotees to perform ritual baths (theertham snanam) that are believed to wash away sins and fulfill wishes. What makes it especially unique is the integration of water‑based processions into daily festival life, blending sacred mythology, visual theatre, and community participation in a way that transforms the temple precincts into a living stage for devotion. The tank, combined with the temple’s rituals and mythic associations, exemplifies how Kapaleeshwarar Temple uniquely merges spiritual, cultural, and ecological symbolism, making it a truly singular place of worship in the Shaiva tradition.

Temple Timings and Rituals

The Parthasarathy Temple typically opens early every morning and remains accessible until the evening, welcoming devotees for daily darshan. Standard hours are about 6 :00 AM to 12 :00 – 12 :30 PM in the morning and around 4 :00 PM to 9 :00 PM in the evening, with slight extensions on Saturdays and festival days. During these times, multiple daily rituals and six traditional poojas are observed, beginning with Suprabhatam (waking hymn) and continuing through Thirumanjanam, Kala Sandhi, Uchikala, and evening Ardha Jamam. These rites involve sacred bathing of the idol (abhishekam), decoration with flowers and silk (alankaram), food offerings (naivedyam), and lamp worship (deepa aradhanai), all enriched with Vedic chants and devotional music that immerse visitors in spiritual ambience.

Festivals

The festival calendar at the Parthasarathy Temple is vibrant and diverse, reflecting its status as a major Vaishnavite centre. The Brahmotsavam during the Tamil month of Chittirai (April–May) is the temple’s grandest festival, featuring elaborate processions of the deity on various sacred vahanams (vehicles) such as Garuda, Hanuman, Horse, and Elephant, along the streets of Triplicane. The temple also holds the Theppam (Float) Festival in Masi (February–March) on its sacred Kairavani tank, where the deity is taken on a beautifully decorated raft for seven days, accompanied by celebrations for other forms of Vishnu like Narasimha, Ranganatha, Rama, and Gajendra Varadar. Other important observances include Vaigasi Utsavam, Varadarajar Utsavam, Nammalwar Utsavam, Vaikunta Ekadasi during Margazhi, and festivals for Ramanuja and Manavala Mamunigal, making its spiritual year rich with devotional intensity.

History

The temple’s origins go back to the 6th century CE when it was constructed by the Pallava kings, with later expansions and renovations by the Cholas and Vijayanagara rulers in the medieval period, reflecting its enduring prominence. Because it is praised in the ancient Divya Prabandham — the Tamil devotional canon composed between the 6th and 9th centuries by the Alvars — the temple has been a living centre of Vaishnavite worship for well over a millennium, making it one of Chennai’s most historically significant temples. Its architectural layout and shrines reflect contributions from successive dynasties that enriched the temple with additional mandapams, gopurams, and sacred spaces.

Darshan & Special Poojas

Daily pujas follow the sacred Vaikhanasa Agama and Thenkalai tradition of Vaishnavism, with ceremonies at set intervals throughout the day. Special poojas and offerings are especially significant on Vaikunta Ekadasi, festival days during Brahmotsavam and Theppam, and on auspicious lunar events, drawing large congregations of devotees. Some devotees also participate in archana (special invocation) and annadhanam (food offering) services, particularly during unique celebrations such as Krishna Jayanthi and Laksharchana, enhancing individual worship experiences.
